Q3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools, and why?

For tickets this simple, I think all three would have been simpler as one agent with the same tools. What stood out most in the run was the redundancy, the back-and-forth between agents on fairly straightforward tasks. Ticket 101 is a straight line: check stock, see that the vendor is blocked, request the payment, draft a message. Ticket 102 is little more than reading the lease (get_lease_rent) and requesting the rent (request_payment). Because each agent starts without the others' context, they kept re-reading the same facts. On 101, the ticket was read 7 times across the agents, and on 103 cash was checked 3 times and stock 4 times. Customer Service also re-verified figures the Boss had already given it, which used all six allowed delegations. Even the one mistake in the run, the unnecessary landlord note on 102, came from coordination between agents rather than from the work itself.

My first guess was that this redundancy is the price of safety. Part of that is true. Splitting the work into roles means only Accounting can request money and only Customer Service writes to customers, and every step leaves a clear "who asked whom" record. But the rules that actually protected the shop are built into the tools and the code: human approval before any payment, amounts taken from the database, no negative cash, and drafts only. Those guardrails would protect a single agent just as well. So the hand-offs made the process much more expensive (71 model calls and about 208,000 input tokens for three simple tickets: 26 calls on 101, 20 on 102 and 25 on 103) without adding much safety beyond them. I think a multi-agent team pays off when tasks are larger, can run in parallel, or need truly different expertise. For a small desk like this one, one agent with the right tools behind the same guardrails would be simpler, cheaper, and just as safe.

Evidence on this page: the “MCP tools actually used” tables on the 101, 102 and 103 tabs (for example, get_ticket ×3, ×2 and ×2 on 101), and the model-call counts in each tab’s run line.

Q4. Three new problems Campus Customs might face that this team couldn't solve, and the tools and agents needed

1. Buying from suppliers on credit. Today the shop can only buy with cash it already has, which is exactly why ticket 103's restock of 12 hoodies ($264) stalled against $160 in checking. Without a fix, that will keep happening whenever cash is tight. Most companies buy from their suppliers on trade credit: they receive the goods now and pay later under pre-arranged contract terms, for example net 30 days, which is typical for small suppliers. To support this, I would add a vendor contracts table with each vendor's credit limit and payment terms, plus a tool to read those terms. A second tool would draft a credit purchase order, a document that promises payment by a set date (today plus 30 days) under the agreed contract. A third would let a person approve the purchase, since nothing should be executed without human approval, just like payments today. A Purchasing agent, or an expanded Accounting agent, would check the terms and the credit limit and prepare the order once Inventory flags a shortfall. After approval, the future bill would join Accounting's payment queue. Purchases on credit would also follow the existing vendor rule: no new credit with a vendor that has an overdue invoice. With this in place, the 103 problem wouldn't come back: the 12 CC-HOOD-NAVY hoodies in size M could have been ordered from Bulldog Print Co on net-30 terms (paying the $264 by 2026-09-30 instead of on 2026-08-31), and all 20 delivered to the Yale AI Club.

2. Customer returns. The team has no way to take anything back. If a customer isn't happy with a product, nobody can receive the item, understand why it came back, and make it right. The shop would first need to record its sales, since today the database has no orders to tie a return to, plus a returns table. The tools would open a return with the customer's reason, record the item when it arrives (putting it back into inventory if it can be resold), issue a replacement after checking stock, or request a refund. A refund takes money out of the shop, so it would need human approval and could never make cash negative, the same rules as every payment. A Returns or Customer Care agent, or an expanded Customer Service agent, would talk with the customer, record why they were unhappy, and offer a replacement first and a refund second. Inventory would handle the replacement and restocking, Accounting would prepare the refund for approval, and the Boss would decide any exceptions. The agent would follow a clear policy: returns within 30 days, for unworn items with their tags, with a replacement offered first and a refund otherwise. For example, if Tauhid Zaman's Classic Bulldog Tee doesn't fit and comes back unworn within 30 days, the agent could swap it for a size M (5 in stock) or, if Tauhid prefers, request a refund of the $28.00 list price for my approval. Over time, tracking return reasons by product would also point to quality problems worth raising with the vendor, such as Bulldog Print Co for the tees and hoodies.

3. Planning financial obligations ahead of time. I think this should be the priority. Right now the team only reacts to bills when a ticket shows up, and nobody looks ahead. After the full run, checking holds $160 while the next $2,400 rent for the Chapel Street shop is due on 2026-10-02, a $2,240 shortfall, and nothing in the system raises that. A real shop also has taxes and regular expenses that the database doesn't track at all. I would add a Finance Planner agent that owns the shop's whole financial calendar: rent, vendor invoices and credit terms, taxes and recurring expenses, each with an amount and a due date. Its tools would list everything due in the next 30, 60 or 90 days, forecast the cash balance against those obligations, raise a warning ticket on the board when a future payment won't be covered, and prepare payment requests ahead of time, still behind human approval. The other agents would ask the Finance Planner instead of each working out cash on its own, for example "Can we commit $264 due on 2026-09-30 without missing the $2,400 rent due 2026-10-02?" This creates a safety mechanism so no obligation gets missed, and it also makes the process more efficient: in our run, cash was checked three times on ticket 103 alone, and a single owner of the financial picture would remove most of that repeated tracking.

Evidence on this page: the Cash tab (ending balance $160.00, restock draft #5 at $264.00 not placed) and the Ticket 103 tab ($104.00 gap between the restock and available cash).

Q5. Three problems the team ran into that the existing tools could have solved

1. The rent due-date confusion (ticket 102). After I approved the rent, the follow-up run saw the lease's next due date at 2026-10-02 while the landlord's notice said 09-02. It treated that as a discrepancy and drafted a note asking the landlord to confirm the date. The answer was already available through our tools. get_ticket(102), which Facilities called in that same run, listed payment request #2 for the rent as paid. list_payment_requests, which the Boss called, showed the same, and list_payments records the rent paid on 2026-08-31. The agents simply didn't connect "the September rent was just paid" with "so the next due date moved forward a month." A prompt rule for Facilities would have prevented it: after rent is paid, next_due moves forward one month, so check the ticket's payment requests before calling anything a discrepancy. So would having the Boss confirm with Accounting before a landlord note goes out.

2. The same ticket re-read again and again (ticket 101). On ticket 101 the agents called get_ticket seven times across the two runs, and on 103 cash was checked three times and stock four times, even though nothing had changed in between. The Boss's very first get_ticket already returned everything the team needed: the linked invoice and vendor, the stock row and the pricing. Passing those facts down in each delegation message, and telling specialists not to re-read what they were already given, would have removed most of those calls. Every extra call re-sends the whole conversation to the model, which makes this redundancy both slow and expensive.

3. Too much back-and-forth between agents (ticket 103). After Accounting had already reported to the Boss that only $160 was free against the $264 restock, Customer Service asked Inventory and Accounting to confirm the same facts again, and Inventory then asked Accounting once more. That used all six allowed delegations and made 103 the most expensive ticket (25 model calls). None of it was needed. The Boss had given Customer Service the final figures. And Inventory's own draft_restock_order result had already said the restock wasn't affordable (affordable_now: false, with the cash available), so it had no reason to ask Accounting. Clearer prompts would have fixed this with the same tools: trust facts handed down by the Boss, only delegate for something nobody has provided, and read your own tool results first. A lower delegation limit would also help.
